Iodine

Lets your thyroid make the hormones that set how your body uses energy.

Daily need (adults)150 mcg a day
Upper limit1,100 mcg
Best foodsIodized salt, dairy, fish
Pay attention ifPregnancy and breastfeeding
FoodsHow muchWho's at riskSigns of low levelsSupplementsFormsMythsFAQ

What it does

  • Lets your thyroid make thyroid hormones
  • Those hormones set your metabolism
  • Needed for a baby's brain and bone development

🤓 Did you know?

The US started iodizing table salt in the 1920s to fight goiter. Manufacturers still do it, though it's voluntary.

👩‍🍳 Kitchen tips

  • Use iodized salt at home, but keep total salt modest. Packaged foods almost always use non-iodized salt.
  • Check your prenatal vitamin if you're pregnant: many contain no iodine.

How much do you need?

Adults150 mcg
Pregnancy220 mcg
Breastfeeding290 mcg

Recommended daily amounts for adults, from the US Food and Nutrition Board.

⚠️ Upper limit

1,100 mcg a day. Too much can cause or worsen thyroid problems, especially if you have autoimmune thyroid disease.

Who's most at risk of running low?

Pregnancy and breastfeedingNeeds rise to 220–290 mcg.
Vegans and people who skip dairy, eggs and seafoodThese are the main sources besides salt.
People who don't use iodized saltIncluding people who switch to sea, rock or pink salt.

Signs you might be low

  • Swelling at the front of the neck (goiter)
  • Tiredness, feeling cold or weight gain (an underactive thyroid)
  • Trouble concentrating

If you have thyroid symptoms, a TSH blood test is the place to start. Iodine levels are usually judged across a population, not in one person.

🩺 When to see a doctor

These signs have many possible causes. If they last more than a couple of weeks, see a doctor. A simple blood test can often check your levels. Don't guess with high-dose supplements.

Supplements: do you need one?

When it helps

If you're planning a pregnancy, pregnant or breastfeeding, the American Thyroid Association recommends a supplement with 150 mcg of iodine a day. Otherwise, iodized salt, dairy and eggs usually cover it.

Choosing one

Potassium iodide is the usual form. Avoid kelp and seaweed supplements: their iodine content varies widely.

💊 Medicine interactions

  • Antithyroid medicines (like methimazole): high iodine can tip you toward an underactive thyroid
  • ACE inhibitors (like lisinopril) and potassium-sparing water pills (like spironolactone): potassium iodide may raise blood potassium

Always tell your doctor and pharmacist about any supplements you take.

Forms of Iodine

Iodine comes from salt, dairy and seafood, and supplements use one main form.

  • Iodine in foodIn food

    Iodized salt, dairy, eggs and seafood. Seaweed varies hugely.

  • Potassium iodideSupplements

    The form recommended in prenatal supplements.

  • Kelp and seaweed supplementsSupplements

    Iodine content varies widely, so they're hard to dose safely.

Myths vs facts

MythBroccoli, cabbage and soy will harm your thyroid.
FactOnly if you're short on iodine. With enough iodine and a varied diet, normal amounts aren't a concern.
MythFancy salts are a better choice.
FactSea, rock and pink salt usually aren't iodized, so switching can leave you short on iodine.

Frequently asked questions

Should I take iodine for my thyroid?

Not without a diagnosis and your doctor's advice. If you have autoimmune thyroid disease, extra iodine can make things worse.

How much iodized salt is enough?

About ¼ tsp of iodized salt gives roughly half a day's iodine. Dairy, eggs and fish cover much of the rest, so there's no need to add extra salt.
