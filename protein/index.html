Protein

Builds and repairs muscle, skin, enzymes and immune cells. Your body can't store extra for later, so you need a steady supply every day.

Tap a type for its best foods, Indian picks and what to watch.

Dairy & eggsComplete protein that's cheap, easy and vegetarian-friendly. Dairy brings calcium too.
Meat & fishThe most protein per bite, plus well-absorbed iron, zinc and vitamin B12.
Dals & beansThe backbone of vegetarian protein: cheap, filling and packed with fiber, iron and folate.
SoyThe plant protein that's complete: all 9 essential amino acids, just like eggs and dairy.
Nuts & seedsProtein with healthy fats. A great add-on, but calorie-dense, so think handful, not bag.
Grains & milletsNot protein stars on their own, but eaten every day, grains quietly add up.
Protein powdersConvenient protein when food falls short. Helpful for some, unnecessary for most.

What it does

  • Builds and repairs muscle, skin, hair and nails
  • Makes enzymes and some hormones that run your body's chemistry
  • Makes antibodies, the proteins your immune system uses to fight infections
  • Carries things around your blood, like oxygen (hemoglobin) and iron
  • Helps you feel full for longer after a meal

🤓 Did you know?

Protein is made of 20 building blocks called amino acids. Your body can make 11 of them. The other 9 are "essential": you have to get them from food. A "complete" protein has all 9 in good amounts.

👩‍🍳 Kitchen tips

  • Aim for a protein food at every meal, not one big serving at dinner. Breakfast is where most people fall short.
  • Upgrade the dal: a thicker dal, a side of curd, or a handful of peanuts in poha can add 5–10 g.
  • Roti or rice with dal is a great team. The grain and the legume cover each other's weak spots.
  • Swap a sugary snack for roasted chana, Greek yogurt or a boiled egg.

How much do you need?

Women (US)46 g a day
Men (US)56 g a day
The formula behind it0.8 g per kg of body weight (0.36 g per lb)
India (ICMR-NIN)0.83 g per kg: about 46 g for women, 54 g for men
Pregnancy (US)71 g a day
Pregnancy (India)Extra 9.5 g a day in the 2nd trimester, 22 g in the 3rd
Breastfeeding (US)71 g a day
Adults over 651.0–1.2 g per kg (expert group advice)
Regular hard training1.2–1.6 g per kg (Australian Institute of Sport)

The US and Indian figures are the amounts that cover the basic needs of almost all healthy adults. The new 2025–2030 US Dietary Guidelines suggest more: 1.2–1.6 g per kg. Experts don't all agree on whether everyone needs that much, but higher amounts are widely seen as reasonable for older and active adults. Example: a 70 kg (154 lb) adult needs about 56 g at 0.8 g/kg, or 84–112 g at 1.2–1.6 g/kg.

ℹ️ Upper limit

No upper limit has been set for protein. The US Food and Nutrition Board suggests protein make up 10–35% of your calories. If you have kidney disease, your doctor may advise less, so follow their plan.

Complete vs incomplete protein

Not all protein is built the same. What matters is the mix of amino acids and how well your body uses it.

  • Complete proteinComplete

    Has all 9 essential amino acids in good amounts. Eggs, dairy, meat, fish, soy and quinoa.

  • Incomplete proteinPlant

    Low in one or two essential amino acids. Most beans, grains, nuts and seeds. Still very useful.

  • Complementary proteinsPlant

    Two foods that fill each other's gaps, like dal and rice or rajma and roti. They don't need to be in the same meal, just the same day.

  • Isolated proteinSupplement

    Protein pulled out of a food and dried into a powder, like whey, casein, soy or pea protein.

Benefits, rated by evidence

🟢Strong evidence🟡Some evidence⚪Not enough evidence🔴MythHow we rate evidence
  • Building muscle with strength training🟢Strong evidence

    A review of 49 trials found protein helped people gain a bit more muscle and strength from weight training. Gains leveled off at around 1.6 g per kg a day.

  • Keeping muscle as you age🟡Some evidence

    Muscle shrinks with age. Expert groups suggest 1.0–1.2 g per kg for adults over 65, together with regular activity. Strength training matters as much as the protein.

  • Feeling full and losing weight🟡Some evidence

    In 24 trials, higher-protein diets during calorie cutting led to slightly more fat loss (under 1 kg on average) and helped keep muscle. Modest, but real.

  • Bones🟡Some evidence

    Protein is part of bone. Studies don't show that plenty of protein harms bones, and soy and animal protein seem about equal for bone density.

  • Recovery after illness or surgery🟡Some evidence

    Needs rise when you're sick or healing. Expert advice for older adults with illness is 1.2–1.5 g per kg. Follow your care team's plan.

Who should pay more attention?

Adults over 65Appetite often drops while protein needs rise.
Vegetarians who skip dal and dairyRice-and-roti-heavy meals can be low in protein. Dal, curd, paneer and soy fix that.
Pregnancy and breastfeedingNeeds rise to about 71 g a day in the US guidance.
People cutting caloriesEating less overall often means less protein, and some of the weight lost can be muscle.
Poor appetite or long illnessIll health raises needs and lowers appetite at the same time.

Protein powders: do you need one?

When it helps

Most people can get enough from food. Indian guidelines from ICMR-NIN advise against protein supplements for healthy people. A powder can help if your appetite is poor, if you can't reach your target with food, or right after hard training when food isn't handy. The Australian Institute of Sport suggests 20–40 g per serve and no more than 1–2 serves a day.

Choosing one

Look for a third-party seal such as NSF Certified for Sport, Informed Choice or USP, which test for banned substances and contaminants. In a 2025 Consumer Reports test, more than two-thirds of 23 powders and shakes had more lead per serving than its experts consider safe for a day. Plant-based powders averaged the most. Check for added sugar too. More on powders →

💊 Health conditions, medicines & cautions

  • Kidney disease: if you have chronic kidney disease (CKD) and aren't on dialysis, too much protein can strain your kidneys. Ask your doctor or a kidney dietitian how much is right for you
  • Levodopa (for Parkinson's): protein can compete with it in the gut, so some people find it works less well after a high-protein meal. Don't cut protein; ask your doctor or dietitian about timing
  • Processed meat (bacon, sausage, ham, salami) is linked to colon cancer. Eating 50 g a day raises the risk by about 18%. Choose fresh, minimally processed protein most of the time
  • Protein powders are lightly regulated. Some contain added sugar, caffeine or heavy metals. Treat them as a food, not a medicine

Always tell your doctor and pharmacist about any supplements you take.

Popular claims we checked

ClaimYou can only absorb about 30 g of protein at a time.
🔴Myth

Your body absorbs nearly all the protein you eat. A 2023 study found 100 g after exercise kept muscle-building going for over 12 hours, longer than 25 g. Spreading protein over the day is still a good habit.

ClaimVegetarians can't get enough protein.
🔴Myth

They can. Dal, beans, soy, paneer, curd, milk, nuts and seeds add up. Variety across the day is what counts.

ClaimYou have to combine plant proteins at every meal.
🔴Myth

An older idea. Eating a variety of plant proteins over the day is enough. Your body keeps a pool of amino acids to draw on.

ClaimHigh-protein diets damage healthy kidneys.
⚪Not enough evidence

There's no clear evidence of harm in people with healthy kidneys. People with kidney disease are different: they often need less, so follow your doctor's advice.

Show all 7 claims
ClaimYou need protein powder to build muscle.
🔴Myth

Powder is just convenient protein. Food works as well. The real driver of muscle is strength training.

ClaimMore protein alone builds muscle.
⚪Not enough evidence

The muscle gains in studies came from protein plus strength training. Extra protein without exercise isn't shown to add much muscle.

ClaimPlant protein is lower quality.
🟡Some evidence

Partly true. Most plant foods are lower in one or two amino acids and are digested a little less well. Eating a mix of plants, or adding soy or dairy, closes the gap.

When to see a doctor

🩺 Book a visit if you have

  • Losing weight or muscle without trying
  • Swelling in your legs or feet
  • Poor appetite for more than a couple of weeks
  • Kidney or liver disease, before changing how much protein you eat
  • Plans to start a high-protein diet if you have diabetes or take several medicines

Tests to ask about

AlbuminA blood protein. Low levels can point to poor nutrition, liver or kidney problems.
Kidney function (creatinine, eGFR)Checks how well your kidneys filter. Useful before a big jump in protein if you have risk factors like diabetes or high blood pressure.
Urine albumin (UACR)Looks for protein leaking into urine, an early sign of kidney strain.
Complete blood count (CBC)Checks for anemia, which can come with a poor diet.

Only your doctor can interpret results. Serious protein deficiency is rare in the US, but running a bit low is common in older adults and people with small appetites. A doctor can check your weight, muscle and blood tests such as albumin and a complete blood count (CBC).

Frequently asked questions

How much protein do I need in grams?

A simple starting point is 0.8 g per kg of body weight. Multiply your weight in kg by 0.8. If you're over 65 or train hard, 1.0–1.6 g per kg is often suggested. Ask your doctor if you have kidney disease.

Is dal and rice enough protein?

It's a good start. A bowl of dal from about 50 g of dry dal gives roughly 11 g, and rice adds a few more. Add curd, paneer, an egg or a handful of peanuts to most meals to reach your target.

What's the best protein for vegetarians?

Soy (tofu, soya chunks, edamame), dals and beans, paneer, curd, Greek yogurt and milk. Mix them across the day.

When should I eat protein after a workout?

Within a few hours is fine. The Australian Institute of Sport suggests about 0.3 g per kg (usually 15–30 g) at each meal or snack, spread over 3–5 eating times a day.

Are eggs good for protein?

Yes. One large egg has about 6 g of complete protein. The yolk holds about 40% of it, so eat the whole egg unless your doctor says otherwise.

Is protein powder safe?

Most are fine in moderation, but quality varies. Pick one with a third-party seal, check for added sugar, and don't rely on it daily if food can do the job.
