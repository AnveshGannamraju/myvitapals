Inflammation

Inflammation is your immune system's repair crew. Short bursts heal cuts and fight germs. The problem is when it never clocks out. Here's what keeps it running, and what actually helps.

The short answer

  • Short bursts of inflammation heal you. The long, low-level kind is the one worth lowering.
  • The biggest levers: move most days, lose weight if you carry extra, and don't smoke.
  • Eat Mediterranean-style: vegetables, dals and beans, whole grains, nuts, olive oil, and fish if you eat it.
  • No supplement, detox or single "superfood" fixes it. Ongoing pain or swelling needs a doctor.

What it is

Inflammation is how your body reacts to injury or infection. Blood flow increases and immune cells rush in to clean up and repair. That's why a sprained ankle swells and a cut turns red.

There are two kinds. Acute inflammation is fast and short: it starts within hours and settles in days. Chronic (long-term) inflammation is low and slow: it simmers for months or years, often with no obvious symptoms.

⚡ Acute: the good kind

Redness, heat, swelling, pain. Lasts hours to days. It's a sign your body is healing. Don't try to "fix" it with food.

🐢 Chronic: the problem kind

Low-level and quiet. Linked with heart disease, type 2 diabetes, some cancers and joint problems. This is what lifestyle changes target.

🏷️ Is it a diagnosis?

"Chronic inflammation" isn't a diagnosis on its own. It's a process. A doctor looks for the cause, such as an infection, an autoimmune condition or a metabolic problem. Be wary of anyone who says you "have inflammation" and sells a cure in the same breath.

What causes chronic inflammation

The badge shows how strong the evidence is that each one drives inflammation.

  • Extra body fat, especially around the waist🟢Strong evidence

    Fat tissue, particularly deep belly fat, releases inflammatory signals. Higher body weight tracks with higher inflammation markers.

  • Smoking and polluted air🟢Strong evidence

    Smoking raises inflammation and lowers the body's natural anti-inflammatory signals. Polluted air and dust at work (like silica) add to the load.

  • Sitting most of the day🟢Strong evidence

    Regular exercise lowers inflammation markers, so long stretches of inactivity leave them higher.

  • Autoimmune conditions and lingering infections🟢Strong evidence

    In conditions like rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, psoriasis or inflammatory bowel disease, the immune system attacks the body's own tissue. Some infections, like TB, can also smolder for months. These need a doctor, not a diet.

  • Short or poor sleep🟡Some evidence

    Irregular sleep and insomnia are linked with higher inflammation markers.

  • Long-term stress and low mood🟡Some evidence

    Ongoing physical or emotional stress triggers inflammatory signals. Depression is linked with higher CRP too.

  • An eating pattern high in sugar, refined flour and trans fat🟡Some evidence

    Diets heavy in sugary drinks, refined carbohydrates (maida, white bread), and saturated and trans fats are linked with more inflammatory molecules.

  • Gum disease🟡Some evidence

    Inflamed, bleeding gums are linked with heart disease and diabetes. The link is well documented, but it isn't yet proven that gum disease causes them.

  • Getting older🟡Some evidence

    Inflammation markers tend to creep up with age. Scientists call this "inflammaging." Staying active and keeping a healthy weight seem to slow it.

What actually helps

Lifestyle first. These are the changes with research behind them, strongest first.

  • Move most days🟢Strong evidence

    Aim for 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, like brisk walking, plus muscle strengthening on 2 days. A review of 83 trials found exercise lowered CRP, even without weight loss.

  • Lose some weight, if you carry extra🟢Strong evidence

    Weight loss is the single most effective way to lower chronic inflammation. Exercise plus weight loss beat exercise alone in the trials above.

  • Stop smoking🟢Strong evidence

    One of the biggest single changes you can make for inflammation and your heart. Ask your doctor about quit aids.

  • Eat Mediterranean-style🟡Some evidence

    Lots of vegetables, fruit, beans, whole grains, nuts and olive oil, with fish if you eat it. In trials it lowered some inflammation markers (IL-6, IL-1β) more than other diets, though the drop in CRP wasn't clear-cut.

  • Sleep 7 or more hours🟡Some evidence

    The CDC recommends at least 7 hours a night for adults. Steady bedtimes help too.

  • Manage stress🟡Some evidence

    Yoga, meditation, a walk with a friend. Pick what you'll actually do. Stress drives inflammatory signals, and calming practices are a sensible, low-risk way to push back.

  • Brush, floss and see the dentist🟡Some evidence

    Treating gum disease is good for your mouth for sure, and may help the rest of you.

💊 A word on painkillers

Anti-inflammatory painkillers like ibuprofen and naproxen are useful for short-term pain. Taken daily for weeks, they can raise heart attack and stroke risk. If you need them most days, see a doctor.

Foods and eating patterns that help

No single "superfood" lowers inflammation. Your overall pattern is what counts.

  • Processed meats like sausages and bacon

Nutrients and supplements

Food first. Talk to your doctor or pharmacist before starting a supplement, especially if you take medicines.

  • Omega-3 fats🟡Some evidence

    EPA and DHA (from fish) and ALA (from flaxseed, chia, walnuts). Trials in rheumatoid arthritis show people needed fewer painkillers, but effects on pain and swelling were inconsistent. Adults need 1.1 g (women) to 1.6 g (men) of ALA a day; 1 tbsp of flaxseed has about 2.35 g. No upper limit is set, and the FDA considers up to 5 g a day of EPA + DHA from supplements safe. High doses can increase bleeding risk, and 4 g a day was linked to a slightly higher risk of an irregular heartbeat (atrial fibrillation) in heart patients. On warfarin? Your doctor may want to check your INR.

  • Vitamin D🟡Some evidence

    In the large VITAL trial, 2,000 IU a day for about 5 years led to 22% fewer new autoimmune conditions (a borderline result). But vitamin D supplements don't reliably lower CRP. If you might be low, ask your doctor about testing. Upper limit: 4,000 IU a day.

  • Fiber🟡Some evidence

    High-fiber diets are linked with lower inflammation. Dals, beans, whole grains, vegetables and fruit are the easy way in.

  • Vitamin C⚪Not enough evidence

    An antioxidant found in amla, guava, citrus and peppers. Food sources are great, but there isn't good evidence that supplements lower inflammation in people who aren't low. Upper limit: 2,000 mg a day.

  • Vitamin E⚪Not enough evidence

    Another antioxidant, found in nuts, seeds and plant oils. Supplements haven't been shown to lower inflammation in people who aren't low, and they can raise bleeding risk with blood thinners like warfarin. Upper limit: 1,000 mg a day from supplements.

  • Magnesium, zinc, selenium⚪Not enough evidence

    Your body needs them, and being low is linked with more inflammation. That doesn't mean extra pills help if your levels are fine. Get them from nuts, seeds, dals and whole grains.

Popular claims we checked

ClaimTurmeric supplements fix inflammation
🟡Some evidence

Cooking with haldi is great. As supplements, turmeric and curcumin show early promise for knee osteoarthritis pain. But high-strength "bioavailable" curcumin products have been linked to liver damage, and supplements may be unsafe in pregnancy. Ask your doctor first, especially if you take medicines.

ClaimDetox teas and juice cleanses flush out inflammation
🔴Myth

There's no good research that detox programs remove toxins. Any weight lost usually comes back. Some products have had hidden ingredients, and colon cleanses can cause real harm. Your liver and kidneys already do the detox job, for free.

ClaimTomatoes, potatoes and brinjal (nightshades) cause inflammation
⚪Not enough evidence

Research hasn't found that nightshades harm joints or raise inflammation. If one seems to bother you, try leaving it out for a few weeks and see.

ClaimDairy is inflammatory
🔴Myth

A review of 52 clinical trials found dairy, including curd and fermented milk, had a mildly anti-inflammatory effect overall. The exception: people with a true milk allergy.

ClaimSugar causes inflammation
🟡Some evidence

Partly true. A diet high in refined sugar and sugary drinks is linked with more inflammation. A sweet now and then isn't the problem; the daily pattern is.

ClaimA high CRP tells you what's wrong
🔴Myth

CRP shows that inflammation is present, not where it is or why. It can be raised by a cold, an injury, smoking, poor sleep or extra weight. Your doctor interprets it alongside your symptoms and other tests.

ClaimPainkillers are a harmless daily fix
🔴Myth

Regular non-aspirin NSAIDs (like ibuprofen or naproxen) can raise heart attack and stroke risk, sometimes within weeks. The FDA advises the lowest dose for the shortest time. Ongoing pain needs a doctor's visit, not a bigger bottle.

When to see a doctor

🩺 Book a visit if you have

  • A joint is hot, red and swollen, or several joints are stiff for over an hour each morning
  • Fever, night sweats or weight loss you can't explain
  • Ongoing belly pain, diarrhea, or blood in your stool
  • A rash with joint pain, or tiredness that lasts more than a few weeks
  • Gums that bleed often or are pulling back
  • You use painkillers most days to get by

Tests to ask about

CRP (C-reactive protein)Shows how much inflammation is present right now, from any cause. Useful for spotting infection or tracking a known condition.
hs-CRP (high-sensitivity CRP)Picks up small rises and is sometimes used to fine-tune heart risk. Under 1 mg/L is lower risk, 1–3 average, over 3 higher. Over 10 usually means a short-term cause, like an infection, so it's retested.
ESR (sed rate)An older, slower marker of inflammation, often ordered with CRP.
Others your doctor may addBlood sugar (HbA1c), cholesterol, vitamin D, or specific tests for autoimmune conditions, depending on your symptoms.

Only your doctor can interpret results. One reading isn't a diagnosis.

Frequently asked questions

What's the fastest way to lower inflammation?

There's no overnight fix. The biggest proven levers are regular exercise, losing weight if you carry extra, and not smoking. Diet and sleep add to it. Expect changes over weeks to months.

Is there an anti-inflammatory diet?

Not one official diet. The Mediterranean-style pattern has the best evidence: vegetables, fruit, dals and beans, whole grains, nuts, olive oil and fish. A typical home-cooked Indian vegetarian meal with dal, sabzi and roti fits nicely; just go easy on fried snacks and sweets.

Should I get an hs-CRP test?

It's not a routine screening test for everyone. Doctors mainly use it to fine-tune heart-risk decisions when your risk is in the middle range. Ask your doctor whether it would change anything for you.

Can I drink haldi doodh (turmeric milk) every day?

Yes, turmeric in food and drinks is fine for most people. The safety questions are about concentrated supplements, not the spice in your kitchen.

Do I need omega-3 capsules if I'm vegetarian?

Not automatically. You can meet the ALA need from ground flaxseed, chia or walnuts. Whether a fish- or algae-based EPA/DHA supplement makes sense for you is a good question for your doctor, especially if you take a blood thinner.

Does fasting or a cleanse reset inflammation?

There's no good evidence that cleanses do this, and some carry real risks. Steady habits beat short resets.
