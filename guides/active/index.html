    Active & working out

    Exercise doesn't dramatically raise your vitamin needs. Eating enough to fuel your training usually covers them. But a few vitamins deserve attention, and some popular supplements can work against you.

    The vitamins to focus on

    Vitamin DSupports muscles and bones. Indoor training and winter months make low levels more likely.
    Vitamin B2B vitamins help turn food into energy. Vegetarian athletes may need a little more riboflavin.
    Vitamin B12Plant-based athletes should plan for B12, since low levels cause fatigue.
    Vitamin CFood first. Some research suggests that high-dose vitamin C pills (around 1,000 mg a day) may blunt training gains.
    Vitamin ESame story: high-dose antioxidant pills may get in the way of your body's training response. Nuts and seeds are fine.

    Fueling tips

    Before trainingA banana or toast: easy carbs, plus some B6
    After trainingCurd, milk, eggs or a chickpea bowl for protein and B vitamins
    Every dayColorful fruit and veg for vitamin C. No megadose needed
    Check labelsPre-workouts often contain B6 and B12. Count them toward your total

    💪 Don't forget protein

    Regular hard training raises protein needs to about 1.2–1.6 g per kg a day. Spread it over 3–5 meals and snacks of roughly 15–30 g each. Food works as well as powders. Protein: needs & best sources →

    📌 Good to know

    Iron, calcium and fluids matter a lot for active people too. Women who train hard are especially prone to low iron, so ask your doctor about a ferritin test if you're often tired.

    🩺 Talk to your doctor about

    • If you train hard and feel unusually tired, ask about vitamin D, B12 and iron tests.
    • Supplements can contain undeclared ingredients. Competitive athletes should look for third-party tested products, like NSF Certified for Sport.
    • Talk to a sports dietitian before taking high-dose antioxidants.
